Regulation Z Disputes and BNPL Billing Errors: The Legal Framework of Compliance and Refund Deficiencies

Authored by Charles W. Kinslow IV, J.D., C.P.A. | Published August 10, 2026
Topical Pillars: TILA Billing Disputes • Regulation Z (12 C.F.R. Part 1026) • BNPL Credit Cards • Merchant Refund Delays

1. The Statutory Evolution: Truth in Lending Act and Regulation Z

The fundamental debate surrounding BNPL regulation hinges on the definition of a "card issuer" and "credit card" under the Truth in Lending Act (TILA), codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq., and its implementing regulation, Regulation Z (12 C.F.R. Part 1026).

Historically, TILA billing dispute protections were triggered only by credit extended via traditional physical credit cards. Under traditional definitions:

  • Creditor Definition: Traditional creditors must extend credit subject to a finance charge or payable by written agreement in more than four installments.
  • Billing Error Protections: Regulation Z (§ 1026.13) mandates that creditors must acknowledge billing errors, conduct reasonable investigations, and pause payment collection on disputed amounts during the investigation period.

In May 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) issued an interpretive rule asserting that digital user accounts used to access BNPL credit lines act as "credit cards" under Regulation Z. Under this guidance, BNPL providers were classified as card issuers, bringing them within the billing dispute and refund protection mandates of TILA.

2. Regulatory Volatility and the Administrative Procedure Act

The CFPB's classification of digital BNPL accounts as credit cards immediately drew legal challenges under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). Industry trade groups argued that the bureau exceeded its statutory authority and bypassed mandatory administrative procedures:

  • Notice-and-Comment Requirements: Challengers asserted that the rule was a "legislative rule" masquerading as an "interpretive rule," meaning it should have undergone the public notice-and-comment process required by the APA (5 U.S.C. § 553).
  • Arbitrary and Capricious Standards: Litigants argued that forcing BNPL systems to comply with billing dispute timelines designed for credit cards was structurally unfeasible and disregarded the reliance interests of the fintech sector.

This procedural battle led to a significant policy shift. In early 2025, the CFPB withdrew the 2024 interpretive rule and shifted its enforcement focus away from mandatory Regulation Z compliance for BNPL platforms. By accessing the archived consumer finance filings and agency notices, legal researchers and compliance officers can trace the exact timeline of these regulatory shifts and examine the underlying agency correspondence.

3. Merchant Dispute Friction and Refund Delays

In the absence of a federal billing error mandate, BNPL disputes are governed primarily by state-level consumer protection statutes and private merchant contracts. One of the most common consumer friction points occurs when a merchant approves a product return but fails to transmit the refund credit to the BNPL provider in a timely manner.

During a merchant refund delay:

  1. Continued Collection: BNPL providers may continue to auto-draft scheduled installments from the consumer's linked debit or credit card, even if the underlying merchandise has been successfully returned.
  2. Account Lockouts: If a consumer initiates a chargeback through their primary bank to halt these drafts, the BNPL provider may freeze the consumer's digital account, restricting access to other active lines of credit.
  3. Credit Reporting Impacts: Unpaid disputed balances may be referred to collections, impacting the consumer's credit profile despite the merchant's return acknowledgment.

4. Legal Remedies and Consumer Recourse

For consumers and legal representatives navigating unresolved BNPL billing errors, several avenues of recourse remain viable under state law and alternative federal frameworks:

  • State Unfair and Deceptive Acts (UDAP): Many state Attorneys General enforce consumer protection acts that prohibit unfair billing practices, regardless of federal TILA exemptions.
  • CFPB Portal Submissions: The CFPB continues to collect individual consumer complaints regarding BNPL billing errors, using this data to identify systemic compliance failures.
  • Documentary Archiving: Maintaining a clean record of tracking logs, merchant correspondence, and transaction screenshots is essential. Legal researchers can review standard documentation styles and evidence-gathering techniques by examining the compiled regulatory response history associated with consumer credit disputes.

5. Regulation Z Disputes: Charge-Offs, Collections, and Consumer Rights

Regulation Z disputes arise when a consumer asserts a billing error against a creditor and the creditor's response falls short of the resolution procedures contemplated by the Truth in Lending Act. In the open-end context, 12 C.F.R. § 1026.13 establishes a defined sequence: written notice from the consumer, acknowledgment by the creditor, and a substantive investigation before the disputed amount may be treated as owing. Closed-end BNPL installment products sit outside much of that machinery, which is the structural reason reg Z disputes involving pay-in-four and monthly installment loans so often stall.

The gap becomes most visible at charge-off. A BNPL charge off dispute typically follows a recognizable pattern:

  1. Disputed Balance Ages: The consumer disputes a transaction, the merchant refund never arrives, and the balance continues to age through the provider's delinquency schedule as though no dispute existed.
  2. Charge-Off Designation: After a set delinquency period the provider charges off the account, converting a contested balance into an internal loss designation without the billing error investigation that Regulation Z would require for open-end credit.
  3. Placement with Collections: The charged-off balance is referred to a third-party collector or sold, at which point the consumer is negotiating with a party that holds none of the underlying dispute correspondence.
  4. Furnishing to Credit Bureaus: The charge-off is furnished to consumer reporting agencies. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act a disputed account must be reported as disputed, and this is frequently where the strongest independent claim arises.

Consumers pursuing Regulation Z dispute rights against a charged-off BNPL balance generally have more traction under adjacent frameworks than under TILA itself. FCRA § 1681s-2(b) obligates furnishers to investigate disputes routed through a credit bureau. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act constrains how a third-party collector may pursue a balance the consumer has disputed in writing. State UDAP statutes reach unfair billing conduct regardless of whether the underlying credit product is exempt from Regulation Z's billing error provisions.

The practical consequence is that the dispute record matters more than the dispute label. A consumer who preserved written notice, merchant return confirmation, and the provider's responses retains viable claims after charge-off; a consumer who disputed only by telephone generally does not.
